## 1. Overview

---

Badminton is often called the fastest racket sport in the world.

At higher levels, players don't wait until they see the shuttle before they start moving.

Instead, they prepare **before** the opponent hits the shuttle.

This preparation consists of three simple but extremely important concepts:

- Ready Position
- Split Step
- Recovery

Every movement you make on court starts with these three.

Whether you are playing:

- a clear
- a smash
- a drop shot
- a net shot
- a defensive block

you will always repeat the same movement cycle.

## 3. The Badminton Movement Cycle

---

Before learning movement, understand this cycle.

```
Recover
        ↓
Ready Position
        ↓
Read Opponent
        ↓
Split Step
        ↓
Move
        ↓
Play Shot
        ↓
Recover
```

Every rally is simply this cycle repeated over and over.

---

## 4. Principle #1

---

> **Always Recover.**

After every shot, your first job is not to admire your shot.

Your first job is to recover and prepare for the next one.

## 5. What is the Ready Position?

---

The ready position is your neutral position before moving to the shuttle.
Think of it as the athletic position used in almost every sport.
A good ready position allows you to move in any direction quickly.

One of the biggest misconceptions in badminton is that there is a single "correct" ready position.

In reality, your ready position changes continuously depending on what is happening during the rally.

For example:

- Waiting in a neutral rally
- Attacking after your own smash
- Defending against an opponent's smash
- Receiving a serve
- Controlling the front court

Each of these situations requires a slightly different stance.

However, they all share the same fundamental principles:

- Stay balanced.
- Keep your weight on the balls of your feet.
- Keep your knees flexed.
- Prepare your racket early.
- Stay relaxed.
- Be ready to move explosively in any direction.

Rather than memorising one fixed stance, your goal is to understand these principles and adapt your position to the situation.

---

### 5.1 Four Common Ready Positions

As you improve, you'll naturally use different ready positions depending on the rally.

In this academy we'll gradually learn all of them.

### a. Neutral Ready Position

Used during normal rallies when your opponent has multiple shot options.

Focus on:

- Balanced stance
- Weight evenly distributed
- Racket in front
- Ready to move in every direction

---

### b. Attacking Ready Position

Used after playing an attacking shot such as a smash.

Characteristics:

- Body slightly forward
- Racket higher
- Weight biased forwards
- Ready to intercept weak returns

---

### c. Defensive Ready Position

Used when expecting a smash.

Characteristics:

- Lower body position
- Wider stance
- Racket lower and further forward
- Ready for fast reactions

---

### d. Service Return Ready Position

Used before receiving serve.

Characteristics:

- Lower centre of gravity
- Dominant foot slightly forward
- Racket held high
- Weight prepared to move forwards

## 6. Principle #2

---

> **Don't commit to a direction before your opponent hits the shuttle.**

One of the biggest mistakes beginners make is guessing where the shuttle will go.

If you commit too early, a good opponent can easily wrong-foot you by changing the direction of their shot.

Instead:

1. Prepare in the appropriate ready position.

2. Perform a well-timed split step.

3. Read the shuttle as your opponent makes contact.

4. Then push explosively towards the shuttle.

Good movement is based on **reading**, not **guessing**.

---

## 7. What is the Split Step?

---

The split step is one of the most important movements in badminton, yet it is often misunderstood.

Many beginners think it is simply a small jump before moving.

It isn't.

The split step is a **timing movement** that connects your **ready position** to your **first step**.

Regardless of whether you're in a:

- Neutral Ready Position
- Attacking Ready Position
- Defensive Ready Position
- Service Return Ready Position

…the split step prepares your body to move explosively in whichever direction the shuttle is played.

Think of it as loading a spring.

Without a split step, your body has to accelerate from a completely stationary position.

With a correctly timed split step, your legs are already loaded and ready to push in the required direction.

Rather than thinking of it as a jump, think of it as preparing your body to react.

---

### 7.1 The Badminton Movement Sequence

The split step is only one part of a complete movement cycle.

Every rally follows the same pattern.

```text
Recover
        ↓
Ready Position
        ↓
Read Your Opponent
        ↓
Split Step
        ↓
Read the Shuttle Direction
        ↓
First Step
        ↓
Move to the Shuttle
        ↓
Play Your Shot
        ↓
Recover
```

As you continue through this academy, you'll notice this sequence repeated in almost every lesson.

The better you become at this movement cycle, the more efficient your footwork will become.

---

### 7.3 Why Does the Split Step Work?

Imagine trying to sprint from a completely still position.

Now imagine taking a small, controlled hop before sprinting.

The second movement feels quicker because your muscles are already prepared to generate force.

The split step works in exactly the same way.

It helps you:

- Store elastic energy in your legs.
- Improve reaction speed.
- Maintain better balance.
- Change direction more quickly.
- Reduce recovery time between shots.
- Move efficiently without unnecessary effort.

The split step isn't about jumping higher—it's about becoming ready to move sooner.

---

### 7.4 The Most Important Part: Timing

A technically perfect split step performed at the wrong time is still ineffective.

The objective isn't simply to perform the movement.

The objective is to **land from your split step at the exact moment your opponent makes contact with the shuttle.**

This timing allows you to:

- Read the shuttle's direction.
- Push off immediately.
- Reach the shuttle earlier.

If you land **too early**, your muscles have already relaxed before you need to move.

If you land **too late**, you'll still be in the air while the shuttle is already travelling.

Neither situation allows you to react efficiently.

> 💡 **Coach's Tip**
>
> The timing of your split step begins by watching your opponent—not the shuttle.
>
> As your opponent prepares to strike, begin your split step so that your feet touch the ground at the exact moment they hit the shuttle.
>
> Learning this timing is far more important than jumping higher or moving faster.

## 8. Principle #3

---

> **Don't move before you split.**

Many recreational players instinctively start running as soon as they think they know where the shuttle is going.

Good players do the opposite.

They:

- Prepare.
- Perform a well-timed split step.
- Read the shuttle.
- Then explode towards it.

The split step provides the stable foundation that allows every first step to be quicker, more balanced, and more efficient.

## 9. Solo Drills

---

### Drill 1 — Ready Position

Stand in ready position.

Hold for 30 seconds.

Relax.

Repeat 5 times.

Focus on:

- staying balanced
- keeping the racket up
- remaining relaxed

---

### Drill 2 — Split Step

Perform:

```
Split Step
  ↓
Recover
```

Repeat

30 repetitions.

No shuttle required.

---

### Drill 3 — Split & Move

```
Split
  ↓
Move Forward
  ↓
Recover
```

Repeat 20 times.

Repeat for:

- backward
- left
- right

## 10. Partner Drill

---

Ask your partner to point randomly to different corners.

Every movement should begin with:

```
Split Step
  ↓
Move
  ↓
Recover
```

The goal is not speed.

The goal is correct movement.

## 11. Common Beginner Mistakes

---

- Standing too upright.
- Jumping too high during the split step.
- Moving before splitting.
- Remaining flat-footed.
- Forgetting to recover.
- Watching the shuttle instead of the opponent.
